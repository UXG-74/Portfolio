03 · Principles

No nonsense. Just good design.

A few things I’ve picked up along the way. Some learned the hard way.

  1. 01 / Question everything.Just because something’s always been done a certain way doesn’t mean it’s right. Challenge assumptions, ask awkward questions and never settle for “that’s how we do things.”
  2. 02 / Keep it simple.Complexity is easy. Making something genuinely simple takes thought, patience and a willingness to strip away the unnecessary.
  3. 03 / Less talk. More doing.Sooner or later, you’ve got to roll your sleeves up, make something and see if it actually works.
  4. 04 / Stay curious. Stay restless.There’s always another way to approach a problem. New tools, new thinking, new possibilities. The day you stop learning is the day you start standing still.
  5. 05 / Care about the details.The little things aren’t little. Spacing, language, interactions, accessibility. It’s the attention to detail that separates something that works from something that feels right.

We only get what we settle for.

    Lucille London

    AI-crafted brand identity.

    A speculative fine lingerie and nightwear brand — invented for this project, with no traditional creative pipeline. Strategy, design language, commerce UX, and the deployed site were built with AI, then iterated until they cleared a believable luxury shelf.

    Lucille London is fiction — it exists only as an AI-assembled portfolio work. The goal was to test whether a full luxury brand and storefront could hold credibility when strategy, visuals, narrative, and build all emerge from the same machine-led workflow. The bar stayed high: editorial and intimate on first impression, then consistent across navigation, merchandising, and reassurance (shipping, care, returns) without the spell breaking.

    The constraint was deliberate: build the narrative, visual system, and product experience with AI as the primary creative accelerator — Cursor to structure and stress-test the experience end to end, Higgsfield for visual exploration, Claude Code for language architecture — so quality came from iteration, not volume.

    Luxury e-commerce is judged in milliseconds: hierarchy, whitespace, and restraint either read as confidence — or as chaos. Every module had to earn its place: hero proposition, collections, edits that behave like a magazine, and product cards that carry craft without doubt.

    The stack was the studio: snapshot passes, variants, microcopy generations — all AI-native. Discipline lived in direction — which prompts survived, which routes shipped, what still scans as intentional when you scroll at 2 a.m. on a phone.

    The outcome is a coherent fiction on a live URL: one voice ("Lucille London"), a collection story that carries from landing to basket, and interaction patterns that respect hesitation in high-consideration categories — without the site reading as a thin brochure.

    OVERVIEW

    Design language that reads as craft, not template.

    Lucille sits where Parisian romance meets London shopping culture: French lace and silk, a collection spine (La Belle Époque), and a retail story rooted in Kensington's boutique reality. The brief wasn't "make a pretty shop" — it was to make trust and desire occupy the same screen.

    Strategy, design, and storytelling were shaped as one system — prompted, critiqued, and refined so the brand could repeat without sounding rehearsed. The goal was cohesion across every touchpoint: the promise in the hero, proof in product detail, and calm confidence through policy and care. Nothing ornamental. Everything accountable.

    THE CHALLENGE

    An AI-crafted luxury shelf has to pass the squint test.

    AI can generate abundance fast. Luxury reads as decision: what you omit, how you pace a page, the words you choose when the garment is tactile but the interface is glass. The challenge: anchor iteration in Cursor (IA, flows, build quality); use Higgsfield for visual direction and exploration; lean on Claude Code for narrative and UI microcopy — and keep the polish human-tight so nothing reads synthetic.

    Every pass moved toward a single identity: editorial typography, warm minimal layout, and shopping paths that reward browsing (New Arrivals, Edits, collections, swim and nightwear) while keeping orientation obvious. Cohesion isn't repetition — it's a recognisable voice that scales from campaign headline to size and care.

    BOUTIQUE EXPERIENCE

    Mood as a product requirement.

    Luxury lingerie is bought with emotion first — confidence, intimacy, occasion — and justified with facts second: fabric, fit, service. The experience needed to evoke a refined, intimate space: unhurried, tactile language, and cues that mirror the Kensington boutique promise without pretending the screen is a fitting room.

    I treated atmosphere as UX: spacious rhythm, restrained merchandising blocks, and a voice that whispers rather than sells. The outcome reads as effortless — even though the system underneath is deliberately structured for scan, compare, and return visits.

    THE LAUNCH

    Commerce designed around intent, clarity, and momentum.

    Launch is where brand meets behaviour. Navigation and collection architecture were built to answer real journeys: discovering what's just arrived, moving between lingerie and nightwear, stepping into swim as a sibling category, and finding gift moments without breaking narrative flow.

    Secondary reassurance — shipping, gift wrapping, returns — was placed to reduce cognitive load at decision time, not buried as legal footnotes at the end. The UI pattern is simple: elevate the collection story, make product evaluation calm, and let service proof support the leap to cart.

    COLOUR PALETTE

    A near-noir canvas with warm neutrals and romantic contrast.

    The palette follows luxury digital conventions, tightened for clarity: deep base tones for drama and focus, off-white surfaces for textile and product truth, and restrained accent used sparingly so promotion and urgency never cheapen craft.

    Colour hierarchy maps to intent — storytelling zones stay quiet so photography carries emotion; interactive states stay legible for accessibility and confidence at checkout. Every swatch earned its place on the page.

    WORDMARK

    Typographic identity over decorative marks.

    The visual system leans on wordmark discipline — Lucille and LONDON in a composed lockup — rather than an illustrated logo zoo. Supporting iconography stays minimal and functional: wayfinding and service cues that disappear until needed.

    The direction favours serif-forward headlines for editorial romance, paired with a clean sans-serif workhorse for wayfinding, pricing, and product metadata — so emotion lives in storytelling, and clarity lives in shopping.

    CAMPAIGN

    Headlines that behave like covers, not banners.

    Campaign moments ("Fall in love with lace", "The art of doing nothing") function as chapter titles: they set tone for a collection and invite scroll without shouting discounts. The same voice carries into editorial Edits — culture, style, gifting — so content reads as part of the product world, not a bolt-on blog.

    AI-assisted drafting produced breadth fast; curation produced the brand. The final lines were chosen for memorability, fit, and a house that sells intimacy with dignity.

    BRAND PACKAGING

    Gifting is part of the product — online and in hand.

    Packaging thinking surfaces where shoppers need reassurance: complimentary gift wrapping, clean delivery expectations, and a premium unboxing story implied by the brand language. Digital "packaging" is the order journey — confirmation, clarity, care instructions — so the experience feels complete when the box arrives.

    The aim is continuity: the same quiet confidence on the PDP should carry through every post-purchase touchpoint, so the brand feels intact from first impression to hanger.

    CONCLUSION

    What this proves.

    AI can accelerate the messy middle — options, variations, microcopy drafts, visual references — but design is still the arbiter of taste, truth, and friction. Lucille London is a case in disciplined iteration: fewer loud claims, more consistent signals, and a store that earns return visits because it feels considered.

    The live site is the artifact — browse La Belle Époque, move through nightwear and swim, and watch how restraint does the selling.

    Electronic components, tools and industrial supplies

    Four in five visits failed to register.

    Distrelec sells electronic components, tools and industrial supplies to engineers and buyers across six EMEA regions. In 2023, 79% of unique visits failed to complete registration.

    Duplicate accounts were splitting order histories and driving up support requests. My brief was to find out why registration was failing and fix it, using evidence rather than opinion, before anything was committed to the roadmap.

    On mobile, half as many made it through.

    GA4 showed me where people were leaving. Over 12 months, 127,286 users began the journey and 59,217 reached the end. On desktop, 51% made it through. On mobile, just 27%.

    I watched the sessions instead of guessing.

    The numbers said where. Hotjar recordings, rage taps and form analytics said why. Three problems kept repeating.

    01

    Email failed late

    Failed email validation was the single biggest trigger for abandonment.

    02

    Key questions came last

    Country and email sat too far down the form, so people hit problems after they’d already invested effort.

    03

    Dead ends at checkout

    A disabled purchase button gave no reason why, and people kept tapping payment logos that did nothing.

    One flow, every kind of account.

    Before touching the UI, I mapped the whole journey: email lookup, separate B2B and B2C paths, validation and every possible account outcome. Edge cases were designed up front, not discovered in production.

    Ask the hard questions first.

    I rebuilt the form around what the research showed. Country and email now come first, so the checks most likely to fail happen before people invest effort. Sign in and Register sit as tabs on one page, which removes a step and makes the choice obvious.

    01

    Inline validation

    Real-time feedback, so errors are fixed as they happen, not after submit.

    02

    Clear signposting

    Plain instructions at every step, written the way support explains it on the phone.

    03

    Region first

    Sending people to the right region up front, so tax, invoicing and terms are right from the start.

    No more dead ends at checkout.

    The payment logos looked tappable, so we moved them into the footer, where they reassure without inviting a click. The disabled button now comes with helper text asking people to choose a payment option, and it switches to the primary style the moment they do.

    One app for the data-obsessed and the just-started.

    Kinetik serves two audiences in one product: athletes fluent in pace, splits and heart rate, and newcomers who feel the effort long before they can read a chart. Most fitness apps design for one and lose the other.

    The brief was to give both the same confidence: a clear promise on the website, proof in the numbers inside the app, and calm guidance through onboarding, device pairing and recovery.

    Training is felt before it’s read.

    This was never a dashboard template. Discovery defined the brief: what people need to see, when they need it and how fast they can act mid-session. Pride, rivalry and doubt come first; pace, splits and anomalies come second. Three principles came out of it.

    01

    Readable in a second

    Progress you can take in between sets, or half awake before the first rep.

    02

    Spoken, not technical

    Terms that sound like a coach talking, not a line lifted from a datasheet.

    03

    Depth on demand

    Dense analytics stay one tap away, so newcomers are never buried in data.

    Structure before style.

    I worked Figma-first: information architecture and a component kit, then wireframes for the dashboard, live workouts, goals and notifications. Only once the structure held did I bring in Cursor and Claude Code, to tighten the flows and microcopy.

    Designed for real mornings, not demos.

    Launch is where ambition meets the real world. I designed for a first week of onboarding, people coming back after time off, and leaderboard checks at thumb speed. Reassurance sits exactly where confidence dips: permissions, retries and restores. Onboarding ended up more than 22 seconds shorter.

    Bluetooth will fail. The design plans for it.

    A website that shows the proof, not the hype.

    The marketing site speaks in the same voice as the app, so the promise on the homepage matches what people see after their first session. Features, training, the community leaderboard and pricing all lead with the real product, not stock claims.

    Navy steady. Emerald electric.

    Emerald marks wins and progress. Navy anchors type and dashboards. Nickel greys carry dense data without washing out the numbers. Sky, Sunrise and Pulse are held back for alerts, rivalry and the moments that need a nudge: controlled voltage, never confetti.

    Data picked the header, not opinion.

    One decision at a time, in the order people make them.

    My hypothesis: if the first third guides people through one decision at a time, in the order they naturally make them, more will reach a plan, the basket and a completed order, because the page stops asking them to work it all out alone.

    Five numbered steps. Try it.

    An anonymised rebuild of my QA’d final design. Colour updates the phone, storage the device price, upfront cost the monthly payment, contract length the plans you can pick, and the data plan the final monthly price.

    Why steps 1 to 4 are already chosen, and step 5 isn’t. Colour, storage, upfront cost and contract length start on the most common choices, so shoppers see a real price straight away and only change what matters to them. The data plan starts empty on purpose: it’s the biggest commitment in the order, so we never choose it for them. Choosing a data amount reveals three tariffs, none pre-selected, and the basket button stays inactive until one is picked. Nobody adds a phone to their basket without knowing exactly what they’re paying for.

    I benchmarked UK networks and European retailers to see how leaders order device and plan choices, and how much they ask before showing a price.     Designed for the moment something goes wrong.

    A guided flow is only as good as its worst moment, so I specified what happens when stock runs out. An unavailable storage option stays selected and shifts to an error state, the product panel explains why, and the basket button is disabled.

    The out-of-stock moment is tracked as its own event in the test, so friction can be measured, not guessed.

    Step labels became short and action-led. “Choose your data plan” replaced a colder heading, “Unlimited data” replaced wording that invited doubt, and “Add to basket” stayed because it says exactly what happens next. Below the fold, the content now runs brand promise, then product proof, then reviews.

    Lucille London

    AI-crafted brand identity.

    ( Services )

    ai & brand commerce

    A speculative fine lingerie and nightwear brand — invented for this project, with no traditional creative pipeline. Strategy, design language, commerce UX, and the deployed site were built with AI, then iterated until they cleared a believable luxury shelf.

    Lucille London is fiction — it exists only as an AI-assembled portfolio work. The goal was to test whether a full luxury brand and storefront could hold credibility when strategy, visuals, narrative, and build all emerge from the same machine-led workflow. The bar stayed high: editorial and intimate on first impression, then consistent across navigation, merchandising, and reassurance (shipping, care, returns) without the spell breaking.

    The constraint was deliberate: build the narrative, visual system, and product experience with AI as the primary creative accelerator — Cursor to structure and stress-test the experience end to end, Higgsfield for visual exploration, Claude Code for language architecture — so quality came from iteration, not volume.

    Luxury e-commerce is judged in milliseconds: hierarchy, whitespace, and restraint either read as confidence — or as chaos. Every module had to earn its place: hero proposition, collections, edits that behave like a magazine, and product cards that carry craft without doubt.

    The stack was the studio: snapshot passes, variants, microcopy generations — all AI-native. Discipline lived in direction — which prompts survived, which routes shipped, what still scans as intentional when you scroll at 2 a.m. on a phone.

    The outcome is a coherent fiction on a live URL: one voice ("Lucille London"), a collection story that carries from landing to basket, and interaction patterns that respect hesitation in high-consideration categories — without the site reading as a thin brochure.

    See the site

    OVERVIEW

    Design language that reads as craft, not template.

    Lucille sits where Parisian romance meets London shopping culture: French lace and silk, a collection spine (La Belle Époque), and a retail story rooted in Kensington's boutique reality. The brief wasn't "make a pretty shop" — it was to make trust and desire occupy the same screen.

    Strategy, design, and storytelling were shaped as one system — prompted, critiqued, and refined so the brand could repeat without sounding rehearsed. The goal was cohesion across every touchpoint: the promise in the hero, proof in product detail, and calm confidence through policy and care. Nothing ornamental. Everything accountable.

    THE CHALLENGE

    An AI-crafted luxury shelf has to pass the squint test.

    AI can generate abundance fast. Luxury reads as decision: what you omit, how you pace a page, the words you choose when the garment is tactile but the interface is glass. The challenge: anchor iteration in Cursor (IA, flows, build quality); use Higgsfield for visual direction and exploration; lean on Claude Code for narrative and UI microcopy — and keep the polish human-tight so nothing reads synthetic.

    Every pass moved toward a single identity: editorial typography, warm minimal layout, and shopping paths that reward browsing (New Arrivals, Edits, collections, swim and nightwear) while keeping orientation obvious. Cohesion isn't repetition — it's a recognisable voice that scales from campaign headline to size and care.

    BOUTIQUE EXPERIENCE

    Mood as a product requirement.

    Luxury lingerie is bought with emotion first — confidence, intimacy, occasion — and justified with facts second: fabric, fit, service. The experience needed to evoke a refined, intimate space: unhurried, tactile language, and cues that mirror the Kensington boutique promise without pretending the screen is a fitting room.

    I treated atmosphere as UX: spacious rhythm, restrained merchandising blocks, and a voice that whispers rather than sells. The outcome reads as effortless — even though the system underneath is deliberately structured for scan, compare, and return visits.

    THE LAUNCH

    Commerce designed around intent, clarity, and momentum.

    Launch is where brand meets behaviour. Navigation and collection architecture were built to answer real journeys: discovering what's just arrived, moving between lingerie and nightwear, stepping into swim as a sibling category, and finding gift moments without breaking narrative flow.

    Secondary reassurance — shipping, gift wrapping, returns — was placed to reduce cognitive load at decision time, not buried as legal footnotes at the end. The UI pattern is simple: elevate the collection story, make product evaluation calm, and let service proof support the leap to cart.

    COLOUR PALETTE

    A near-noir canvas with warm neutrals and romantic contrast.

    The palette follows luxury digital conventions, tightened for clarity: deep base tones for drama and focus, off-white surfaces for textile and product truth, and restrained accent used sparingly so promotion and urgency never cheapen craft.

    Colour hierarchy maps to intent — storytelling zones stay quiet so photography carries emotion; interactive states stay legible for accessibility and confidence at checkout. Every swatch earned its place on the page.

    WORDMARK

    Typographic identity over decorative marks.

    The visual system leans on wordmark discipline — Lucille and LONDON in a composed lockup — rather than an illustrated logo zoo. Supporting iconography stays minimal and functional: wayfinding and service cues that disappear until needed.

    The direction favours serif-forward headlines for editorial romance, paired with a clean sans-serif workhorse for wayfinding, pricing, and product metadata — so emotion lives in storytelling, and clarity lives in shopping.

    CAMPAIGN

    Headlines that behave like covers, not banners.

    Campaign moments ("Fall in love with lace", "The art of doing nothing") function as chapter titles: they set tone for a collection and invite scroll without shouting discounts. The same voice carries into editorial Edits — culture, style, gifting — so content reads as part of the product world, not a bolt-on blog.

    AI-assisted drafting produced breadth fast; curation produced the brand. The final lines were chosen for memorability, fit, and a house that sells intimacy with dignity.

    BRAND PACKAGING

    Gifting is part of the product — online and in hand.

    Packaging thinking surfaces where shoppers need reassurance: complimentary gift wrapping, clean delivery expectations, and a premium unboxing story implied by the brand language. Digital "packaging" is the order journey — confirmation, clarity, care instructions — so the experience feels complete when the box arrives.

    The aim is continuity: the same quiet confidence on the PDP should carry through every post-purchase touchpoint, so the brand feels intact from first impression to hanger.

    CONCLUSION

    What this proves.

    AI can accelerate the messy middle — options, variations, microcopy drafts, visual references — but design is still the arbiter of taste, truth, and friction. Lucille London is a case in disciplined iteration: fewer loud claims, more consistent signals, and a store that earns return visits because it feels considered.

    The live site is the artifact — browse La Belle Époque, move through nightwear and swim, and watch how restraint does the selling.
